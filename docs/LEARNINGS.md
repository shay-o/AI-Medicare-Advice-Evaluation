# Learnings from building and auditing this evaluation harness

A record of what this project has actually taught, including the parts that went wrong. Written 2026-08-17, covering work from January through August 2026.

Most of these came from failures rather than successes, and several came from failures in the auditing itself. That is the point: the interesting content of an evaluation project is usually the part where the measuring instrument turned out to be broken.

---

## 1. The evaluator needs evaluating, and it will not tell you it is wrong

The project's headline claim was that AI models answer Medicare questions more accurately than trained human counselors, 65% versus 37% on the study's top rubric category. The number was produced by a working pipeline, backed by stored transcripts, and reproducible from a manifest.

It was also, in part, wrong. A deliberate pass to check whether the *grader* was right found that on one question every model giving the correct answer was marked `incorrect`, and a model giving the wrong answer was marked `accurate_complete`.

Nothing in the pipeline surfaced this. Verdicts had rationales, aggregates were stable, the report regenerated byte-identically. A broken grader produces clean-looking numbers, which is precisely what makes it dangerous. The only thing that caught it was reading individual verdicts and asking whether they were defensible.

**Transferable:** an LLM-as-judge pipeline needs a judge-validation step as a first-class part of the process, not an afterthought. Budget for reading raw verdicts.

## 2. Diagnose the mechanism, not the symptom

The first investigation correctly identified that the scenario file contained invented plan data contradicting the real plan. It concluded that this bad data was the answer key corrupting grading, and proposed two remediation designs built on that conclusion.

The conclusion was wrong. The grader never receives that field. `grade_run()` takes question text and response text and nothing else, and the grader module contains no reference to plan data at all. Correcting those values could not have changed a single verdict.

The actual answer key was a hardcoded string in the rubric criteria, which the grader does read. Two real defects existed side by side, and the investigation attached the symptom to the wrong one because both involved wrong plan facts.

**Transferable:** "I found bad data and bad behaviour, and the bad data explains the bad behaviour" is a hypothesis, not a finding. Trace the actual code path before writing the remediation plan. A confident, well-evidenced, wrong diagnosis is a normal outcome and worth planning for.

## 3. Rubrics transcribed from a study carry invisible assumptions

The scoring criteria came from the source study's appendix. One read: *"accurate_complete: Counselor says yes (plan includes prescription drug coverage)."*

That is not a general rule about Medicare. It is a fact about the specific plan the original mystery shoppers asked about. When this project substituted a different real plan, one that has no drug coverage, the criterion silently inverted from correct to false. Nothing flagged it, because the text still reads like a reasonable rubric.

**Transferable:** when reusing an instrument, separate its general logic from its instance-specific bindings. Anything phrased as a fixed answer rather than a comparison against ground truth is a binding, and it does not survive changing the instance.

## 4. Establishing ground truth is genuinely hard, and that is the finding

Correcting the plan data meant determining what a real Medicare Advantage plan actually covers. That turned out to be difficult in an instructive way:

- Medicare.gov Plan Finder, the source the rubric names, could not be reached programmatically. One mirror returned HTTP 403, another timed out.
- Two aggregator sites reported the plan **includes** Part D drug coverage. Three other sources said it does not. The two were wrong, because a templated benefits table renders a generic "prescription drugs" row regardless of the plan, contradicting the same site's own prose.
- The plan was marketed under one name in 2025 and a different name in 2026, so a question naming the plan may not identify a single product across years.
- The standard Part B premium changes annually, so any answer key encodes a plan year whether or not it says so. The previous key silently held a 2024 figure.

This is not incidental to the research question. It is the research question. The models fail the plan-specific questions for the same reasons: the authoritative source is hard to query, secondary sources disagree, plan identity is unstable across years, and the facts expire annually. A human counselor with Plan Finder open has a real advantage here, and it is not an advantage of intelligence.

**Transferable:** when an evaluation includes questions whose answers require live lookup, the difficulty of obtaining ground truth is itself a measurable property of the domain, and worth reporting rather than hiding behind a tidy answer key.

## 5. Giving a judge ground truth can contaminate what it thinks it read

The fix for the plan-specific questions was to supply the grader with verified plan facts so it could evaluate correctness against reality instead of memory. This worked for the original defect and introduced a new one.

A model answered: *"I cannot provide the specific premium for the Aetna Medicare Eagle PPO as Medicare Advantage plan premiums vary by location..."*

The grader wrote: *"The response provided accurate information about the $0.00 monthly premium... The response also provided the accurate Part B premium amount of $202.90."*

The response contains neither figure. Both came from the facts block in the grader's own prompt. In the aggregate the signature was obvious once looked for: `not_substantive` verdicts fell from 7.8% to 0.0%, with all 33 upgraded. Models that explicitly refused to answer were being scored as though they had answered correctly.

The prompt merged two operations that must stay separate: establishing what the response actually claims, and checking those claims against ground truth. Ground truth may judge correctness. It must never supply content.

The fix was to separate the operations: reframe the facts as a correctness reference the responder never saw, require the grader to quote the response verbatim before scoring, and store those quotes so verdicts can be audited. That worked. Refusals are scored as refusals again, and the headline moved from the contaminated 72.8% to 63.9%.

**Transferable:** any context added to a judge's prompt is context the judge can mistake for the thing it is judging. Adding reference material to improve accuracy is a change that itself requires validation, and the failure mode inflates scores, which is the direction least likely to prompt suspicion. Forcing the judge to quote the source text before scoring is a cheap and effective guard.

**A second-order lesson:** the fix changed grading for *every* question, not just the affected ones. The general-rules slice fell 4.7 points despite no rubric change and no relevance to plan facts, because requiring verbatim quotes made the grader stricter everywhere. So the final comparison measures two changes at once and cannot isolate the original defect. When fixing a measurement instrument, change one thing at a time, or accept that the resulting delta is uninterpretable.

## 6. Measure the noise floor before believing any delta

Seven verdicts changed on question groups where no rubric had changed and the added plan facts were irrelevant. Same grader, same responses, different run: roughly 7 of 108, about **6.5%** churn.

That is the noise floor. Any re-grade difference smaller than about 6% is indistinguishable from re-running the same grader twice. Without that figure, a 3-point improvement reads as a result rather than as noise.

It was discovered by accident, as a by-product of unchanged questions moving. It should be measured deliberately: grade the same set twice with no changes and record the disagreement rate.

**Transferable:** report an LLM judge's self-disagreement rate alongside its verdicts. It sets the resolution limit of every comparison built on it.

## 7. Selection rules are load-bearing, so gate on a known answer

A re-grade was run over what appeared to be the reported responses. It produced 251 rows and an old-grading baseline of 49%, against a published 180 rows at 65%.

The cause was skipping one filter, `allowed_scenario_ids`, which excludes partial and exploratory runs. The project's own documentation warned that a naive pass yields roughly 47% rather than 65%, and the warning was reproduced almost exactly.

The recovery was to require the recomputation to reproduce the *published old* aggregate before its *new* aggregate was believed. With the filter restored: n=180, 19 question groups, 65.0/25.0/7.8/2.2, matching exactly. Only then was the new number meaningful, and only then was it possible to see that the new number was wrong for a different reason.

**Transferable:** when recomputing a published metric, first reproduce the published value through the new code path. A pipeline that cannot reproduce the old number has not earned the right to report a new one.

## 8. Evaluation targets decay faster than the evaluation

Six months after the runs, four of the nine evaluated models no longer exist on the routing provider, and a fifth is listed but returns no endpoints. The exact model set behind the published figures can no longer be re-run.

This is not a flaw to apologise for. It is a real property of evaluating commercial AI systems, and it is the strongest possible argument for the project's existing "snapshot-based evaluation" principle and its append-only run manifest. Results are timestamped observations about specific model versions, and treating them as durable facts about "AI" would be a category error.

**Transferable:** record exact model identifiers and dates, keep raw outputs, and expect that reproduction will eventually mean "here is what we captured" rather than "run it again."

## 9. A cheap judge adjudicating expert facts is a design smell, and it was measurable

This was tested rather than asserted. A gold set of 18 unambiguous items was built from the reported responses, and three grading models were run across two harness conditions, three replicates each, at temperature 0.

| Model | No plan facts | Grounded harness | Input $/M |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| claude-3-haiku (incumbent) | 63.0% | 85.2% | 0.25 |
| gemini-3-flash | 100.0% | 100.0% | 0.50 |
| claude-sonnet-4.5 | 100.0% | 100.0% | 3.00 |

Every failure in the experiment came from haiku. Three results stand out.

**Model choice dominated harness quality.** Swapping the model with no prompt change moved accuracy 37 points. **A better harness partially rescued the weak model but did not substitute for a good one**: grounding lifted haiku 22 points, and grounded haiku still sat 15 points below either stronger model running bare. **The cheap strong model matched the expensive one**, with gemini-3-flash equalling sonnet-4.5 at a sixth of the price.

The harness still earns its place, just not on accuracy. Both strong models were already at ceiling, so grounding could not raise them, but it improved sonnet's self-consistency from 94.4% to 100% and it is what prevents the contamination failure in section 5.

The uncomfortable implication: at 63% bare, the incumbent grader was wrong on roughly one unambiguous item in three, and it graded every figure this project has published.

**Transferable:** treat judge selection as an empirical question with a measurable answer, not a cost decision. Test model and harness as separate factors, because the cheap fix and the effective fix may not be the same one. And check for the cheap-strong-model case: the price ladder and the quality ladder are not the same ladder.

**A gold-set audit trick fell out of this.** One item was failed by all three models in all six cells. That was not three models being wrong, it was the label being wrong. Unanimous disagreement between independent models is a good signal to re-examine the gold label first. Correcting it moved both strong models from 94.4% to 100%. Note also that 1 of 18 gold labels, 5.6%, was wrong on first construction, which is a reminder that the reference standard needs the same scrutiny as the thing being measured.

The underlying reason: for rubric application over supplied criteria a small model is defensible, but for questions whose answers depend on real-world plan details the task quietly becomes "recall specific commercial insurance product attributes from training data," which is exactly what the models under test were failing at. The grader's contradictory verdicts on neighbouring questions were the visible symptom of asking it to do something it could not do.

## 9c. Upgrading one component exposes the faults the weak one was hiding

Switching to the better grader immediately surfaced a defect that had nothing to do with graders. Responses were being scored against the wrong rubric entirely, because the rubric groups key off the study's question numbers while the scenario files key off conversation turns, and the two drift apart wherever a scenario adds a turn the study does not number: a location reply, or the second half of a two-part question.

A location statement was being graded as a long-term-care answer. A question about Medicaid paying Medicare premiums was graded against the Spanish-translation rubric. Six of ten dual-eligible turns were misrouted.

The weak grader never complained, because it was not good enough to notice that the response in front of it had nothing to do with the criteria it had been handed. It just scored something. The strong grader wrote "the response fails to address the primary topic of the Question Group" and was right.

There is a structural tell that would have caught this without any grader at all. With a correct mapping the per-group counts come out exactly regular: one row per model per group, and double for the single group that appears in both scenarios. Before the fix, 45 rows carried a literal `ERROR` group and the counts were lumpy. Nobody had looked.

**Transferable:** when you improve one component of a pipeline, expect it to reveal faults elsewhere rather than simply raising the score, and budget for that instead of treating it as a setback. And check the cheap structural invariants of your own data: regular counts, no error rows, every expected cell populated. Those catch whole classes of defect before any model is involved.

## 10. Documentation rots toward confidently wrong

Separately from the grading work, an audit of the repository found that every document described a CLI flag that had been renamed, so no documented command would run. The quickstart pointed at a scenario file that did not exist. A guide documented a `--grade` flag that no longer existed and described grading as opt-in when it had become mandatory, inverting its central explanation. The README promised a no-API-key path that could not work, because grading always runs and defaults to a provider requiring a key.

Roughly half the repository consisted of dated development-session reports that no longer described the system.

None of this was flagged by anything. Docs fail silently, and they fail toward confident wrongness rather than obvious absence.

**Transferable:** treat documented commands as testable artifacts. A doc that cannot be executed is worse than no doc, because it consumes trust before it fails.

## 11. Separating the model from the product

The published results show a consistent split: AI beats the human baseline substantially on general Medicare rules, and loses on plan-specific lookups. That is not one finding, it is two populations of question.

The human counselors had Medicare Plan Finder open. A raw API call has no tools at all. Comparing them on lookup questions compares a person with a database against a model working from memory.

This suggests a three-tier design rather than a single comparison: human counselors with tools, AI products with retrieval, and raw API models closed-book. The interesting claim is then sharper and more defensible: on general rules the model alone already outperforms counselors, while on plan-specific facts the surrounding product matters more than the model.

**Transferable:** when a benchmark shows a bimodal result, check whether the two modes correspond to different task types with different tooling assumptions before averaging them into one number.

---

## What still stands

The general-rules questions, 12 of 19 groups, were never affected by any of this. They contain no plan-specific answer key, and re-grading moved them by 0.9 points, well inside the noise floor. That slice is the durable result.

The plan-specific questions are where every defect lived, and they remain unresolved pending the contamination fix.

## What this says about the practice

The strongest artifact this project has produced is not the accuracy number. It is the audit trail: a documented case of an evaluation harness catching its own grader being wrong, misdiagnosing the cause, correcting the diagnosis, introducing a new defect while fixing the original, and catching that too.

That sequence is normal. Publishing it is not, and that is what makes it worth publishing.
